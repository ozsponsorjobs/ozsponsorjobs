Skilled Occupation Lists Explained: CSOL, MLTSSL, STSOL and ROL (2026)

Australia’s skilled visas run on occupation lists — and the lists changed in December 2024. Here is which list controls which visa, how ANZSCO codes work, and what to do if your occupation is not listed.

Updated: November 2026 Author: OZ Sponsor Jobs Editorial Team Reviewed by: Australian Migration Policy Specialists Legal Basis: Migration Regulations 1994; Migration (Specification of Occupations) Instruments
✔ Direct Answer (BLUF)

Since 7 December 2024, the list that matters most for employer-sponsored visas is the Core Skills Occupation List (CSOL) — 456 occupations that control the 482 Core Skills stream and the 186 Direct Entry stream. The older lists — MLTSSL, STSOL and ROL — still exist and still control the points-tested 189, 190 and 491 visas and the regional 494 visa. Your eligibility is decided by your ANZSCO code, not your job title, and some CSOL occupations carry caveats that limit when they can be used.

1. Why Occupation Lists Matter

Australia’s skilled visa system is built on occupation lists. Each visa subclass is tied to one or more lists, and your nominated occupation must appear on the list for that visa — matched by its ANZSCO code — or the nomination cannot succeed. No amount of experience, English ability or employer enthusiasm changes that. The lists are how Australia decides which skills it will sponsor from overseas, and they are the first thing to check before you spend months applying for jobs.

The lists changed on 7 December 2024, when the Skills in Demand visa replaced the old Temporary Skill Shortage visa. Before that date, the 482 and 186 visas used the MLTSSL, STSOL and ROL. After that date, they use the new Core Skills Occupation List. The older lists did not disappear — they still control other visas — which is why so many guides now mix up the old and new systems. This guide keeps them straight.

2. The CSOL: The List That Matters Most

The Core Skills Occupation List (CSOL) is a list of 456 occupations launched on 7 December 2024. It was built by Jobs and Skills Australia from labour market analysis — which occupations are in shortage, which are in demand, and which Australia wants to sponsor. It controls two visas: the Core Skills stream of the subclass 482 Skills in Demand visa, and the Direct Entry stream of the subclass 186 Employer Nomination Scheme visa. If your occupation is on the CSOL, both of those routes are open to you (provided you meet the other requirements: experience, English, salary and age). If it is not, neither is — for that occupation.

The CSOL is broader than the old lists in some ways and narrower in others. It consolidated the occupations from the MLTSSL, STSOL and ROL that Jobs and Skills Australia assessed as genuinely in demand, and it dropped occupations that were on the old lists but no longer in shortage. That is why an occupation that was sponsorable in 2023 may not be sponsorable in 2026, and why an occupation that was never on the old lists may now be on the CSOL. Always check the current list — never rely on a guide written before December 2024.

The CSOL is also the list that matters for the salary floor. The Core Skills Income Threshold (CSIT) of $79,423 a year applies to 482 Core Skills and 186 nominations — the two streams the CSOL controls. The threshold is explained in CSIT Explained, and the 482 process in How to Find a 482 Visa Sponsorship Job in Australia (2026 Guide).

3. The Older Lists: MLTSSL, STSOL and ROL

Three older lists still exist, and they still control several visas. Understanding them matters because they are the route for occupations that are not on the CSOL, and because they control the points-tested visas that many job seekers apply for alongside employer sponsorship.

MLTSSL — Medium and Long-term Strategic Skills List

The MLTSSL covers occupations in medium- and long-term demand. It is the only list for the points-tested subclass 189 (Skilled Independent) visa, and it is also one of the lists for the 190, 491 and 494 visas. Occupations on the MLTSSL are the ones Australia expects to need for years, not just this year.

STSOL — Short-term Skilled Occupation List

The STSOL covers occupations in short-term demand. It does not lead to the 189 visa, but it does lead to the state-nominated 190, the regional 491 and the employer-sponsored regional 494. An occupation on the STSOL but not the MLTSSL can still be sponsored — through the 494, or through a 190 or 491 nomination.

ROL — Regional Occupation List

The ROL covers occupations that are in demand in regional Australia but not necessarily in the big cities. It applies to the 491 and 494 visas only. Together, the MLTSSL, STSOL and ROL cover around 650 occupations for the 494 — noticeably more than the CSOL’s 456 — which is why the 494 is often the route for occupations the CSOL leaves out.

The table below is the whole system at a glance. Find your visa, then find its list.

Two rows deserve attention. The 482 Specialist Skills stream and the 186 TRT stream have no occupation list at all — the first because it is controlled by salary, the second because the occupation is the one your 482 already covered. And the 189 is the only visa that uses the MLTSSL alone. The three visas are compared in 482 vs 186 vs 494: Which Australian Work Visa Fits You?

5. ANZSCO Codes: The Code Is What Counts

Every occupation on every list is identified by an ANZSCO code — a six-digit number from the Australian and New Zealand Standard Classification of Occupations. The code, not your job title, decides whether your occupation is listed. Two jobs with similar titles can have different ANZSCO codes, and two jobs with different titles can share one code. What matters is the duties: the code matches when the tasks you actually perform match the tasks in the ANZSCO description for that occupation.

There are two versions of ANZSCO in use, and which one applies depends on the visa. The 482 and 186 visas use ANZSCO 2022; all other skilled visas use ANZSCO 2013. The two versions differ in some occupation codes and descriptions, so an occupation that is listed under ANZSCO 2022 for a 482 may have a different code — or a different status — under ANZSCO 2013 for a 494. Always check the list for the visa you are applying for, using the ANZSCO version that visa uses.

Getting the code wrong is one of the most common reasons nominations fail. If your employer nominates you under the wrong ANZSCO code, the nomination will be refused even if the job is real and the salary is right. If you are unsure which code fits your duties, a registered migration agent can confirm it — you can check an agent is registered on the MARA website.

6. Caveats: The Fine Print

Being on the CSOL is not always enough. Some CSOL occupations carry caveats — conditions that limit when the occupation can be used. A caveat might require a minimum salary above the CSIT, restrict the occupation to certain business sizes or locations, or exclude certain types of work within the occupation. If your nomination falls inside a caveat, it will be refused even though the occupation is on the list.

Sources disagree on exactly how many CSOL occupations carry caveats, so we do not state a number here. What matters is the check: when you look up your occupation on the Home Affairs skilled occupation list, read the caveat column for your entry. If there is a caveat, read it in full and confirm that your role, your salary and your employer meet it before you apply.

7. Your Occupation Is Not on the List

If your occupation is not on the CSOL, the 482 Core Skills stream and the 186 Direct Entry stream are closed to you for that occupation. That is not the end of the road. Check the other routes in this order:

  • Check the MLTSSL, STSOL and ROL. If your occupation is on any of them, the 494 regional visa is open to you — provided you are willing to live and work in regional Australia. The 190 and 491 points-tested visas may also be options.
  • Check the 482 Specialist Skills stream. If your salary is at least $146,576 a year and your occupation is in ANZSCO Major Groups 1, 2, 4, 5 or 6 (managers, professionals, community and personal service workers, clerical and administrative workers, or sales workers), the Specialist Skills stream can sponsor you without any occupation list. Trades, machinery operators, drivers and labourers are excluded.
  • Check Designated Area Migration Agreements (DAMAs). A DAMA can cover occupations that are not on any list, with concessions on English or salary that the standard streams do not offer. The occupation and the concessions differ by region, so check the agreement for the area. Our DAMA hub lists the regional agreements.
  • Check whether a different ANZSCO code fits. Your duties may match a different occupation that is listed. This is a genuine possibility, not a trick: if the duties in your reference letters match another occupation’s ANZSCO description, that occupation may be the right one to nominate under. Get a registered migration agent to confirm it.

What you should not do is pay anyone who promises to get your occupation “added to the list” or to sponsor you in an occupation you have never worked in. Occupations are added to lists by the Government, not by agents, and a nomination in an occupation you have no experience in will fail. How to verify an employer is covered in How to Check If an Australian Employer Is a Genuine Standard Business Sponsor.

8. How to Check Your Occupation

A five-minute check that saves months of wasted applications.

  1. Find your ANZSCO code. Search the skilled occupation list on the Department of Home Affairs website using your exact occupation. Compare the duties in the ANZSCO description with the duties in your work references — the duties decide the code, not the title.
  2. Check the CSOL entry. Confirm your code is on the Core Skills Occupation List, and read any caveat on the entry.
  3. Check the other lists. If the occupation is not on the CSOL, check the MLTSSL, STSOL and ROL for the 494, 190 and 491 routes.
  4. Match the list to the visa. Use the table in section 4 to see which visas your occupation opens.
  5. Check the salary floor. Confirm the salary for the role clears the CSIT ($79,423 a year for nominations lodged from 1 July 2026) and the market rate — see CSIT Explained.
  6. Apply where sponsors hire. Browse current roles by visa subclass on the jobs board: 482 jobs, 186 jobs and 494 jobs. You can also check whether your occupation is in demand with the eligibility calculator on this site.
