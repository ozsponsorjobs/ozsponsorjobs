How to Check If an Australian Employer Is a Genuine Standard Business Sponsor

Before you accept a sponsored job, verify the employer. There is no public list of approved sponsors — so here is what you can actually check, and the warning signs that mean walk away.

Updated: November 2026 Author: OZ Sponsor Jobs Editorial Team Reviewed by: Australian Migration Policy Specialists Legal Basis: Migration Act 1958; Migration Regulations 1994
✔ Direct Answer (BLUF)

A Standard Business Sponsor (SBS) is a business approved by the Department of Home Affairs to sponsor overseas workers. There is no live public register of approved sponsors, so you cannot look an employer up on a government list. What you can do: check the business on ABN Lookup and the ASIC register, check the Sponsor Sanctions register, and ask the employer directly whether it is approved and whether it has sponsored before. Never pay for a sponsorship, and never trust a “guaranteed visa”.

1. What a Standard Business Sponsor Is

A Standard Business Sponsor (SBS) is a business that the Department of Home Affairs has approved to sponsor overseas workers for the subclass 482 Skills in Demand visa. Approval lasts five years and costs the employer $420. To become a sponsor, a business must be lawfully operating in Australia, have no adverse information against it, and meet training and other requirements. Once approved, the sponsor can nominate workers for specific positions — and each nomination is assessed separately.

Sponsorship is only the first of three steps. After the employer is approved as a sponsor, it must lodge a nomination for your specific position (including labour market testing and the salary tests), and only then do you lodge your visa application. An employer that is an approved sponsor has still not sponsored you until the nomination for your role is approved. That distinction matters: “we are an approved sponsor” is a good sign, but it is not a job offer and it is not a visa.

There is also a higher tier: accredited sponsors. These are sponsors with a strong record of sponsoring workers, and their nominations are processed faster. Accredited status is granted by the Department, and it is a genuine mark of a serious sponsor — but, like standard sponsorship, it is not published on a live public list. The full 482 process is explained in How to Find a 482 Visa Sponsorship Job in Australia (2026 Guide).

2. The Hard Truth: No Public Register

Many job seekers search for a government list of approved sponsors. As at November 2026, the Department of Home Affairs publishes no live public register of approved Standard Business Sponsors. There is no official page where you can type in an employer’s name and see whether it is approved. Any website that claims to show the “official list of all sponsors” is not showing a live government register.

What does exist is a set of freedom-of-information (FOI) releases. The newest complete official list of all active Standard Business Sponsors is an FOI release covering sponsors active as at 30 April 2025 (reference FA 25/05/01968), and the newest list of accredited sponsors covers sponsors accredited as at 15 January 2025 (reference FA 25/01/01229). Both are snapshots, not live lists: a business approved after those dates will not appear, and a business whose approval has since ended may still appear. In July 2026, Home Affairs refused FOI requests for a current list. New legislation — section 140GD of the Migration Act, added by the Migration Amendment (Combatting Migrant Exploitation) Act 2026 — would allow the Department to publish a register of sponsors, but as at November 2026 no such register has been published.

That gap is exactly what scammers exploit. Because you cannot verify sponsorship with one search, you have to verify the business itself and ask the employer the right questions. The next section shows how.

3. The Checks You Can Actually Do

Work through these checks before you accept an offer or share identity documents. Each one takes a few minutes, and together they catch most scams.

Check the business on ABN Lookup

Every genuine Australian business has an Australian Business Number (ABN). Search the business on ABN Lookup, the Australian Government’s free register. Match the legal name, not the trading name: a business can trade under a different name, but the legal entity name is what matters. Check that the ABN is active, that the entity type matches what the employer claims (a company, a sole trader, a trust), and that the business is registered for GST if it claims to be a substantial employer. No ABN, an inactive ABN, or an ABN registered to a different name is a serious warning sign.

Check the ASIC register

If the employer is a company, check it on the Australian Securities and Investments Commission (ASIC) register. Confirm the company is registered, when it was registered, who its directors are, and whether it is in administration or deregistered. A company registered last month that claims ten years of sponsorship history is lying. A company with no directors listed, or with a history of deregistration and re-registration, deserves a second look.

Check the Sponsor Sanctions register

The Australian Border Force publishes a Sponsor Sanctions register listing businesses that have had their sponsorship approval cancelled or been barred from sponsoring. Search it for the employer’s name. A business on the sanctions register cannot sponsor you, and a business that has been sanctioned before is a risk even if it is currently approved.

Ask the employer directly

A genuine sponsor will answer these questions without hesitation. Ask: Are you an approved Standard Business Sponsor? Have you sponsored workers before, and in which occupations? Are you an accredited sponsor? What is the ANZSCO code for the role you want to nominate me in? What is the salary, and does it meet the Core Skills Income Threshold of $79,423 a year and the market rate? An employer that cannot name the ANZSCO occupation, cannot state the salary, or gets defensive when asked about sponsorship is not a sponsor you want. The salary rules are explained in CSIT Explained.

Check the agent on the MARA register

If a migration agent is involved, check them on the register of the Migration Agents Registration Authority (MARA). Only registered migration agents can legally give migration advice in Australia. Ask for the agent’s MARA registration number and check it on the MARA register before you pay anything or share documents. An unregistered “agent” or “consultant” who charges for migration advice is breaking the law.

Verify the visa itself in VEVO

Once a visa is granted, you can check it yourself in VEVO (Visa Entitlement Verification Online), the Department’s official system. A “visa” that cannot be checked in VEVO is not a visa. And remember: Australian visa applications are lodged in ImmiAccount, the Department’s official system. Nobody can grant you a visa through WhatsApp, Telegram or email.

4. Using This Site’s Sponsors Directory

Our sponsors directory lists Australian employers drawn from live job listings. It is a useful starting list: an employer that has advertised sponsored roles before is more likely to sponsor again. But be clear about what it is and is not. The directory is built from job advertisements, and we do not verify any employer’s sponsorship status. Every profile honestly states that its Standard Business Sponsor status is not verified.

Use the directory to find employers to approach, then run the checks in section 3 on each one before you apply. A directory entry is a lead, not a guarantee — and no directory, including ours, can replace the checks above. The same honesty applies to the jobs board: listings are current roles from employers that sponsor, but you should still verify each employer yourself.

5. Red Flags

Any one of these is a reason to stop and verify before you pay anything or share identity documents. Two or more means walk away.

  • You are asked to pay for the job or the sponsorship. Sponsorship ($420), nomination ($330) and the Skilling Australians Fund levy ($1,200 or $1,800 a year) are the employer’s costs by law and cannot be recovered from you. Any request for payment for a job offer, a sponsorship or a nomination is a scam.
  • There is no real business. No ABN, no website, no physical address, no presence on LinkedIn or Google Maps, and contact only through WhatsApp or Telegram.
  • No written contract. A genuine employer gives you a written employment contract with the salary, the occupation, the hours and the conditions. A “job” with no contract is not a job.
  • A visa is “guaranteed”. Nobody can guarantee a visa outcome. Only the Department of Home Affairs decides, and only after sponsorship, nomination and your application have all been assessed.
  • The job was never advertised. A genuine nomination requires labour market testing: the employer must have advertised the role for at least 28 days, normally on Workforce Australia plus two other national platforms, within four months before the nomination. A role that was never advertised cannot be nominated.
  • The salary is below the floor. A 482 Core Skills offer below $79,423 a year (for nominations lodged from 1 July 2026) cannot be approved. A salary quoted without superannuation, or with housing or a car counted as pay, is a warning sign.
  • The employer cannot name the ANZSCO occupation. Every nomination is for a specific occupation with an ANZSCO code. An employer that cannot tell you the code has not thought about the nomination — or is not planning one.
  • Pressure to pay quickly. “Pay today or the offer expires” is a scam tactic. A genuine employer gives you time to check, to read the contract, and to get advice.

6. If You Suspect a Scam

Stop. Do not pay anything, and do not share identity documents, passport scans or bank details. Save every message, email and advertisement from the employer or agent — screenshots with dates are the most useful evidence. If you have already paid, contact your bank immediately and ask about reversing the payment.

Report suspected migration fraud to the Australian Border Force, and report the scam to Scamwatch (the Australian Competition and Consumer Commission’s scam reporting service). If a migration agent was involved, complain to the Migration Agents Registration Authority. Reporting helps other job seekers: the same scammer usually targets many people with the same offer.

If you are already in Australia and your employer is not paying the salary in your nomination, or is asking you to repay sponsorship costs, seek advice from a registered migration agent or the Fair Work Ombudsman. Your visa depends on your employer meeting its sponsorship obligations, and there are protections for workers who report exploitation.
