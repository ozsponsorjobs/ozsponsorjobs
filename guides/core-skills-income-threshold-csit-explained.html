Core Skills Income Threshold (CSIT) Explained: The $79,423 Minimum Salary for Sponsored Workers

If you are chasing a sponsored job in Australia, one number decides whether your nomination can succeed. Here is what the CSIT is, how it replaced the TSMIT, what counts towards it, and the second salary test most job seekers miss.

Updated: November 2026 Author: OZ Sponsor Jobs Editorial Team Reviewed by: Australian Migration Policy Specialists Legal Basis: Migration Regulations 1994, reg 5.42A
✔ Direct Answer (BLUF)

The Core Skills Income Threshold (CSIT) is the minimum salary for a 482 Core Skills or 186 nomination: AUD $79,423 a year for nominations lodged between 1 July 2026 and 30 June 2027. It replaced the old TSMIT on 7 December 2024. Your guaranteed annual earnings must be at least that amount and at least the market rate for the role. The Specialist Skills stream has a higher threshold of $146,576. The figure is indexed every 1 July, so always check the one in force on the day your nomination is lodged.

1. What the CSIT Is

The Core Skills Income Threshold (CSIT) is a legal minimum salary for employer-sponsored workers. When an Australian employer nominates you for a subclass 482 (Skills in Demand) visa in the Core Skills stream, or for a subclass 186 (Employer Nomination Scheme) visa, the salary in the nomination must be at least the CSIT. If it is below the threshold, the nomination will be refused — no matter how good the job match is, and no matter how much the employer wants to hire you.

The threshold exists to protect both you and the Australian labour market. It stops employers from using sponsorship to bring in workers at wages below what Australians earn, and it stops sponsored workers from being underpaid once they arrive. That is why the CSIT is a floor, not a target: most sponsored roles pay well above it, and the market rate test (section 4) often sets a higher effective minimum than the CSIT itself.

The threshold applies to your guaranteed annual earnings — the salary your employer guarantees to pay you in the nomination. It is assessed at the time the nomination is lodged, so the figure that matters is the one in force on that day, not the one in force when you first applied for the job. 2. From TSMIT to CSIT

If you have read older guides, you will have seen the figure $73,150 and the name TSMIT. Both are out of date for the 482 and 186 — but the name still causes confusion, because the TSMIT has not disappeared entirely.

Until 7 December 2024, the salary floor for sponsored workers was called the Temporary Skilled Migration Income Threshold (TSMIT), and it was $73,150 a year. On 7 December 2024, when the old Temporary Skill Shortage visa became the Skills in Demand visa, the threshold for the 482 and 186 was renamed the Core Skills Income Threshold (CSIT), starting at the same $73,150. The TSMIT did not vanish: it still applies to the subclass 494 regional visa (and the 187 Temporary Residence Transition stream), at the same dollar figure as the CSIT. So today, $79,423 is both the CSIT (for 482 and 186) and the TSMIT (for 494) — same number, different names, different visas.

The threshold is indexed every 1 July in line with average weekly ordinary time earnings (AWOTE), under Migration Regulations regulation 5.42A. That is why the figure rises each year, and why any guide quoting $73,150 as the current figure is at least two years out of date. A small number of sources report slightly different 2026 figures ($79,499 for the CSIT and $146,717 for the SSIT); the figures used here — $79,423 and $146,576 — are the ones in the majority of sources, including the Department of Home Affairs salary-requirements page checked in October 2026.

The Specialist Skills Income Threshold (SSIT) is the higher floor for the 482 Specialist Skills stream — the stream for highly paid specialists in professional, managerial, community-service, clerical/administrative and sales occupations. If your salary is at least $146,576, the Specialist Skills stream can sponsor you even if your occupation is not on the Core Skills Occupation List. For most job seekers, though, the CSIT is the number that matters.

4. The Second Test: Market Salary Rate

Clearing the CSIT is necessary but not enough. Your salary must also be at least the annual market salary rate — what an equivalent Australian worker earns for the same job, in the same location, with the same experience. If the market rate for your role in Sydney is $95,000, a $79,423 offer fails even though it clears the CSIT. The Department checks both tests, and failing either one means the nomination is refused.

The market rate is worked out from what the employer pays its own Australian workers in the same role, or — if there is no equivalent Australian worker — from market evidence such as salary surveys, job advertisements and enterprise agreements. Location matters: the same occupation can have a different market rate in Sydney, in Perth and in a regional town. This is the test that catches low offers in high-paying occupations, and it is the reason a salary at exactly the CSIT is often not enough for professional roles.

The market rate also shows up earlier, in the employer’s labour market testing. When the employer advertises the role (at least 28 days, on Workforce Australia plus two other national platforms, within four months before the nomination), the advertisement must show the salary — unless the salary is above $96,400 a year, in which case it can be omitted. If you see a sponsored role advertised without a salary, the salary is above $96,400.

5. What Counts Towards the Threshold

The threshold is measured against your guaranteed annual earnings: the base salary and any guaranteed allowances your employer commits to pay you in the nomination. What does not reliably count is non-guaranteed pay — overtime and weekend penalty rates in particular. An employer cannot offer you a $70,000 base salary and promise that overtime will take you over $79,423. The guaranteed figure must clear the threshold on its own.

Superannuation is separate. Your employer must pay superannuation — Australia’s compulsory retirement contribution — at 12% of your ordinary time earnings since 1 July 2025, and it is paid on top of your salary, not deducted from it. A $79,423 salary therefore means at least $9,530.76 a year in superannuation on top. Superannuation is not counted towards the CSIT, and it is not part of your guaranteed earnings for the threshold test. If you leave Australia permanently after your visa ends, you may be able to claim your superannuation back through the Departing Australia Superannuation Payment, with tax withheld.

Non-cash benefits — a car, housing, or meals — do not count towards the threshold either. If an employer quotes you a package that includes housing or a car and calls the total “$80,000”, check the cash salary in the contract: it is the cash figure that must clear $79,423.

6. What If the Offer Is Below the Threshold?

If the guaranteed salary in your nomination is below the CSIT in force on the day the nomination is lodged, the nomination will be refused and your visa application cannot succeed. There is no discretion and no exception for the Core Skills stream — the threshold is a hard legal floor. The only honest fixes are to raise the salary, to wait for the next 1 July indexation (which raises the threshold, so this does not help a low offer), or to look at a different visa.

A different visa can help in one specific case. If your occupation is covered by a Designated Area Migration Agreement (DAMA), the agreement may include a salary concession that lets the employer nominate you below the standard threshold. DAMA concessions differ by region and by agreement, so check the specific agreement for the area. Our DAMA hub explains the regional agreements.

Once you are in Australia, your employer must actually pay you at least the salary in the nomination. Paying less breaches the employer’s sponsorship obligations. If your pay drops below the nominated salary — for example, if your hours are cut — tell your employer in writing, keep your payslips, and seek advice from a registered migration agent or the Fair Work Ombudsman. Never agree to a lower salary in exchange for sponsorship: it is unlawful for a sponsor to pass its sponsorship costs on to you, and a salary below the threshold puts your visa at risk.

7. What This Means for You

As a job seeker, the CSIT gives you a quick filter. Before you apply for a sponsored role, check the advertised salary: if it is below $79,423 a year (for nominations lodged from 1 July 2026), the role cannot lead to a 482 Core Skills or 186 nomination at that salary. If the salary is not shown, the role pays above $96,400 (the labour market testing disclosure threshold), so it clears the CSIT comfortably. When you get an offer, ask for the salary in writing and check it against both the CSIT and the market rate for the role in that location — salary surveys and job advertisements for the same occupation are a good guide.

Timing matters too. Because the threshold is indexed every 1 July, a salary that clears the threshold in June may not clear it in July. If your nomination is lodged just before 1 July, the old figure applies; if it is lodged on or after 1 July, the new figure applies. Employers know this and usually lodge before the change, but if your nomination slips past 1 July, check that the salary still clears the new threshold.

Finally, remember that the threshold is only one part of the cost of sponsoring you. Your employer also pays $420 for Standard Business Sponsorship (five years), $330 for the nomination, and the Skilling Australians Fund levy — $1,200 a year for a business with turnover under $10 million, or $1,800 a year for larger businesses. Those costs belong to the employer by law and cannot be recovered from you. Browse current roles by visa subclass on the jobs board: 482 jobs, 186 jobs and 494 jobs.
